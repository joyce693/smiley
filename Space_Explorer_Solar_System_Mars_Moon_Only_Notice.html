Surveyor 3: The Robot Humans Visited on the Moon

Surveyor 3 was an uncrewed NASA spacecraft sent to the Moon to photograph the surface and investigate lunar soil. Its landing site later became a destination for Apollo 12 astronauts, who visited the robot and brought selected parts back to Earth.

MISSION STORY

What was Surveyor 3 designed to do?

Before astronauts landed on the Moon, NASA needed close-up images and engineering information about the lunar surface. Surveyor 3 carried cameras and a surface sampler to observe the terrain, examine the soil and send information back to Earth.

After launch on 17 April 1967, Surveyor 3 reached the Moon on 20 April. It landed in Oceanus Procellarum, a broad dark plain on the near side of the Moon. During touchdown it bounced twice before settling on the surface.

ENGINEERING & SCIENCE

How did the robotic soil sampler work?

Surveyor 3 carried a Soil Mechanics Surface Sampler (SMSS), a mechanical arm with a small scoop. The arm could press into the ground, scrape and move lunar material, and help scientists observe how the soil behaved.

  • The scoop investigated the consistency and strength of lunar soil.
  • It dug four trenches, each reaching roughly seven inches (about 18 centimetres) deep.
  • The sampler pressed into the ground and moved soil to help researchers study its mechanical properties.
  • The lander returned more than 6,000 photographs of the lunar surface and its equipment.
Why was this important? Engineers needed evidence about how lunar soil behaved under the feet and landing systems of spacecraft.
THE END OF THE MISSION

What happened at lunar sunset?

Surveyor 3 operated during the lunar daytime. As sunset approached at its landing site on 3 May 1967, the mission’s first lunar-day operations ended. The spacecraft was not designed to keep working through the extremely cold lunar night.

A HISTORIC RETURN VISIT · NOVEMBER 1969

Why did Apollo 12 astronauts visit Surveyor 3?

Apollo 12 landed on 19 November 1969, about 535 feet (163 metres) from Surveyor 3. Astronauts Pete Conrad and Alan Bean walked to the robotic lander, photographed it and examined how it had changed after more than two years on the Moon.

The astronauts removed Surveyor 3’s television camera and selected soil-sampler components and returned them to Earth. Scientists examined the equipment in laboratories to learn how long exposure to the lunar environment had affected it.

WHY SURVEYOR 3 MATTERS

A robot explored the Moon — and humans came back to visit it

Surveyor 3 was an important step in NASA’s robotic exploration of the Moon. Its photographs, soil tests and returned hardware helped scientists and engineers understand the lunar surface and how equipment changed during long exposure there. Its story links robotic missions that prepare the way with human missions that build on what robots discover.

🌕 What is the Lunar Roving Vehicle?

The Lunar Roving Vehicle (LRV), often called the Moon Buggy, was a small electric vehicle used by Apollo astronauts to travel across the Moon. It allowed crews to visit places farther from their lunar landing spacecraft and bring scientific samples back more efficiently.

Think about it: How much more could astronauts explore if they could ride instead of walking everywhere?

🔬 Science and discovery

SCIENCE SPOTLIGHT

Lunar Laser Ranging

How do scientists use the reflector to measure the distance to the Moon?

01 · SEND

A telescope on Earth sends a very short laser pulse towards the Moon.

02 · REFLECT

The corner-cube prisms in the retroreflector send some of the light back towards Earth.

03 · DETECT

Scientists detect returning photons and measure the round-trip travel time.

04 · CALCULATE

The measured time and the speed of light are used to calculate the distance.

DISTANCE FORMULA
d = (c × t) ÷ 2

d = distance · c = speed of light · t = round-trip time

3.8 cm per year

The Moon is gradually moving away from Earth at approximately 3.8 centimetres each year, mainly due to tidal interactions.

The retroreflector is passive: it has no batteries or wires. Measurements are made during observing sessions, and precision depends on equipment and observing conditions.

🔬 What did ALSEP investigate?

  • Moonquakes and impacts using seismometers.
  • Heat moving from the Moon's interior using heat-flow experiments.
  • Magnetic fields, solar wind and properties of the lunar environment using specialised instruments.

The Apollo laser retroreflectors were separate experiments, not part of ALSEP. They are passive mirrors that can still be used for lunar laser-ranging measurements.

🔬 What did Sojourner teach us?

  • It demonstrated that a small, remotely operated rover could travel across the Martian surface.
  • Its cameras and instruments examined nearby rocks and soil.
  • Its Alpha Proton X-ray Spectrometer (APXS) helped scientists study the chemical composition of rocks and soil.
  • The mission helped develop technologies and operational experience used by later Mars rover missions.

Sojourner's findings contributed to understanding Mars' geology. They should not be described as direct proof that liquid water once flowed across the planet.

🔬 What did the twin rovers discover?

  • Spirit: a damaged wheel exposed bright, silica-rich soil. This was evidence of past water-related, possibly hydrothermal activity.
  • Opportunity: found small iron-rich spherules nicknamed “blueberries” and minerals that formed in the presence of water.
  • Both: found geological evidence that ancient Mars had environments very different from the cold, dry world seen today.

The evidence supports water-related processes in Mars' past. It does not mean either rover directly proved that life existed on Mars.

🔬 What did Viking discover?

Viking’s biology experiments produced puzzling chemical reactions, but the mission did not find clear evidence of living microorganisms. The landers also studied Martian weather, surface materials and geology, and returned the first detailed panoramas from the surface.

🔬 What did the landers reveal?

  • Phoenix: confirmed water ice just beneath the Martian surface and studied arctic soil chemistry and weather.
  • InSight: recorded 1,319 marsquakes and helped scientists determine the structure of Mars' crust, mantle and liquid iron core.
  • Both: showed how stationary landers can reveal important information about the surface and interior of another world.

Phoenix's mission ended as the northern winter reduced sunlight and temperatures. InSight stopped communicating after dust reduced the power its solar panels could generate.
